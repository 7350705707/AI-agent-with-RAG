### From general knowledge (Indian context)

A “hardware spare” in cyber‑security usually refers to a **malicious physical device that mimics legitimate hardware**, such as a USB stick, dongle or network adapter. In the case of *hardware spear‑phishing*, an attacker inserts this device into a victim’s system during a targeted phishing operation.

**How it works**

1. **Targeted insertion** – The attacker knows the victim will use a specific device (e.g., a USB flash drive) because of their role or workflow.  
2. **Malicious payload** – When the device is plugged in, the hardware triggers an embedded micro‑controller that runs code without needing OS privileges, evading many software‑based detections.  
3. **Data exfiltration / persistence** – The malware can steal credentials, copy files to a remote server, or create back‑doors for later exploitation.

In India, USB‑based attacks have been reported in corporate espionage and ransomware campaigns, especially against government agencies and large enterprises that rely heavily on mobile workstations. The “spare” aspect means the device is not a standard legitimate USB stick but one that the victim expects to be genuine, making it an effective spear‑phishing vector.

**Key points**

- **Spear‑phishing**: Highly targeted, exploiting specific knowledge about the victim’s habits or devices.  
- **Hardware Trojan**: The device itself is a Trojan; it can store data, run code, and persist even after being unplugged (unlike USB sticks that erase evidence).  
- **Advantage**: It can bypass software anti‑malware because the attack occurs at the hardware level.  

Thus, “hardware spare phishing” is a subset of hardware‑based Trojan attacks used in spear‑phishing to compromise victims with minimal detection.

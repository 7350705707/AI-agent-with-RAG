**Assistant:** **Article 219 – Simple Indian‑Constitution Explanation**

- **What the article says:**  
  “Every person appointed to be a Judge of a High Court shall, before he enters upon his office, make and subscribe before the Governor of the State (or a person the Governor appoints) an oath or affirmation according to the form set out in the Third Schedule.”

- **Who is covered?**  
  Any judge who has been given a seat on one of India’s three High Courts – Bombay, Calcutta (Kolkata) and Delhi.

- **What must happen first:**  
  The new judge cannot start working until he swears an oath. This oath is written in the exact wording and format that the Constitution provides (the Third Schedule).

- **Why it matters for India:**  

  1. **Political independence** – By swearing before the Governor, the judge’s loyalty is bound to the state, not to any political party. The governor cannot later force a judge to act against the law.  
  2. **Uniformity** – All High Court judges follow the same oath, so there is no inconsistency in how they begin their duties.  
  3. **Constitutional safeguard** – It shows that the judiciary is separate from the executive (the Governor) and cannot be used as a political weapon.

- **In plain terms:**  
  Think of it like a soldier being sworn into an army before his first shift. The judge swears “I will do my duty only, I swear by the Constitution,” in front of the state’s chief executive (the Governor). Only after that oath does he become officially on duty.

So, Article 219 simply says: *Before a High Court judge can start working, he must take an oath before the Governor (or a person appointed for him) as required by the Third Schedule.*
